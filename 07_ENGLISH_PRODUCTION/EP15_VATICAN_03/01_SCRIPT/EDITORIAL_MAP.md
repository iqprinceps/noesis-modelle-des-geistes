## Viewer promise

A document hands one man Rome, Italy and the western world in the voice of the
Emperor Constantine. It is a forgery, it worked for centuries, and a scholar
brought it down with a single Persian noun. The viewer is invited to find the
word before Valla does.

## Structural change against the German master

The German version promised a fingerprint and then deflated it at the moment of
delivery: `the single word is not Valla's whole proof`. Three changes:

1. **The reveal is played straight.** The accumulation of further clues follows
   as escalation rather than as retraction of the reveal.
2. **The anachronism is made physical.** Satrap is named as a Persian provincial
   title, with a same-shape modern comparison, so the viewer hears the error
   instead of being told it exists.
3. **The Vatican Library irony is promoted to payoff.** Vat. lat. 5314 sitting
   digitised in a papal collection closes the episode rather than trailing at S8.

## Story movements

1. The claim at full size, then the forgery, then the promise of one word.
2. Why Constantine is the perfect voice: Nicaea, the hinge everyone recognises.
3. What the text does: turns a present claim into an ancient one. Paleae.
4. Two corrections stated positively: not the only pillar, and long contested.
5. Valla around 1440 and the method: a forger cannot forget his own century.
6. The hunt. CONSUL, SENATE, SATRAP. Viewer decision, then the reveal.
7. Escalation: titles, later Latin, and the baptism collision with Eusebius.
8. False and effective. What a document needs in order to be given power.
9. Valla has a present too. One sentence, then straight on.
10. Payoff: Vat. lat. 5314, digitised, open, and still requiring you to know where to look.
11. Series form named, then the printing-press handoff into 1948.

## Interaction

Placed at movement 6 as a three-way identification rather than an opinion poll:
`CONSUL, SENATE, SATRAP`. The viewer performs the episode's method before it is
explained, which is the strongest available form of in-story participation.

## Primary visual anchors

- the printed 1922 Coleman page as a full object first, then the located passage;
- medieval Sylvester and Constantine cycle as the legend layer, clearly dated later;
- authentic Constantinian coinage and portrait sculpture as the material layer;
- Valla manuscripts, marginalia and the early printed edition as the forensic layer;
- the three layers must remain visually separable at all times.

## The satrap pages are located

Acquired 2026-09-03. The Coleman 1922 edition is downloaded in full as PDF plus
OCR text, from the University of Michigan scan at the Internet Archive, item
`aga5892.0001.001.umich.edu`. Public domain in the United States; record the
exploitation-territory check at picture lock rather than relying on the year.
Hashes in `02_SOURCES/COMMONS_ASSET_MANIFEST.csv` and the file header.

The edition prints the Latin and Coleman's English on facing pages, which gives
the episode two distinct assets instead of one:

**Printed pp. 12 and 13. The forgery's own words.** The Donation text reads
`Utile iudicavimus una cum omnibus satrapis nostris, et universo senatu
optimatibusque meis`, with the facing translation `We, together with all our
satraps, and the whole senate and my nobles`. This is the page for the viewer
hunt. The anachronism is visible in the document itself, in Latin, with an
English rendering beside it, before Valla is mentioned.

**Printed pp. 86 and 87. Valla's attack.** `Quis umquam satrapes in consiliis
Romanorum nominari audivit?` Who ever heard of satraps being named in the
councils of the Romans. This is the reveal page.

Show pp. 12 and 13 first, run the CONSUL, SENATE, SATRAP decision on the
document's own sentence, then cut to pp. 86 and 87 for the answer. The episode
gains a real forensic gesture: the viewer reads the evidence before the
investigator does.

Coleman's front matter also lists `satraps (as a Roman official)` among the
distinctive vocabulary markers, which supports the argument without narration.
