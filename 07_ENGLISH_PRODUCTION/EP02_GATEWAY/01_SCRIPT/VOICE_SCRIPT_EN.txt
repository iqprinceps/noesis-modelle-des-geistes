Three observers.

One target.

One sees it now.

One searches the immediate past.

One searches the immediate future.

Their descriptions are compared only after the session.

This was not written for a science-fiction film.

It is Recommendation H in a report prepared for the United States Army.

June ninth, 1983.

Written by Lieutenant Colonel Wayne M. McDonnell.

Fort Meade, Maryland.

The declassified copy now carries CIA archive release marks.

That is why the internet calls it the CIA Gateway Report.

But this was an Army analysis, not a CIA-authored report.

And Recommendation H was not McDonnell's strangest proposal.

He also wanted personnel prepared for possible encounters with intelligent, non-corporeal energy forms.

How did an intelligence officer arrive there?

The path begins with two tones in a pair of headphones.

It ends with time treated like a military coordinate.

And hidden in the middle is a test with ten digits.

The result was too weak to prove the claim.

And, to McDonnell, interesting enough not to discard.

The first man in the chain was Robert Monroe.

Monroe had built a successful career in radio broadcasting.

Then he began describing episodes in which he seemed to observe his own body from outside it.

He did not respond like a mystic.

He responded like an engineer.

He built a laboratory.

At the Monroe Institute in Virginia, recordings, spoken instructions, and numbered mental states became a training system.

Monroe called it Hemi-Sync.

McDonnell was asked to assess how it might work and whether it could be useful.

To build an explanation, he turned to a man who had already been dead for four years.

Itzhak Bentov was an inventor.

His work contributed to the steerable cardiac catheter.

He also believed the human body behaved like an oscillating system.

The heart and aorta created rhythm.

The body entered resonance.

And consciousness, he proposed, could ride that resonance into a larger structure of reality.

On May twenty-fifth, 1979, Bentov boarded American Airlines Flight 191 in Chicago.

The flight was bound for Los Angeles.

Moments after takeoff, the aircraft lost an engine and crashed.

Four years later, his model reappeared inside an Army analysis.

Monroe supplied the training.

Bentov supplied the proposed physics.

McDonnell connected them.

At first, the mechanism is real and modest.

Play four hundred hertz in the left ear.

Four hundred and ten in the right.

The listener perceives a pulse of roughly ten beats per second.

No speaker produces that pulse directly.

It emerges as the auditory system processes the difference between the tones.

That is a binaural beat.

Monroe proposed that these beats could help guide the brain toward particular patterns.

If the hemispheres became more coherent, perhaps unusual states became easier to reach.

Monroe had patented a related sound-based sleep technique in 1975.

Up to this point, the document is talking about sound, attention, electrical activity, and altered state.

Then the report changes scale.

Watch the leap.

The body oscillates.

The brain produces electrical patterns.

Synchronization makes the patterns more coherent.

And then, McDonnell proposes, sufficiently coherent consciousness may connect with a field of information in which space and time do not behave as they do in ordinary perception.

The page still looks like engineering.

Arrows.

Axes.

Vortices.

Nested systems.

Army letterhead.

But the report has crossed from acoustics into cosmology.

There are three ways to read this crossing.

One: McDonnell built a speculative metaphor and began treating it like a mechanism.

Two: the language reflects a military era willing to investigate any possible intelligence advantage.

Three: unusual experiences persisted often enough that he believed the model deserved a test.

The document does not tell us which interpretation is correct.

It does show what he did next.

He drew a map.

Focus 10.

Mind awake.

Body asleep.

Focus 12.

Awareness beyond the ordinary senses.

Then comes the heading that changes the file.

Focus 15.

Travel into the Past.

McDonnell imagines time as a giant wheel.

The hub is the present.

The spokes extend toward points in a person's past.

He also admits that the state is extremely difficult to reach.

McDonnell wrote that fewer than five percent of participants in any given Gateway training achieved it fully.

Then comes Focus 21.

The Future.

Immediately afterward, the report gives physical instructions for leaving the body.

Roll out.

Rise upward.

Move through an opening.

The language reads like a field manual for an impossible maneuver.

But an intelligence officer eventually has to ask a harder question.

Can it retrieve information?

This is where the ten digits appear.

A computer-generated sequence is placed at another location.

Nobody in the room knows it.

Participants are supposed to read the sequence while out of body.

According to a Monroe Institute trainer cited by McDonnell, some obtained enough matching digits to convince him their attention had reached the lab.

Nobody obtains all ten.

That result is the center of the episode.

Not a clean success.

Not a clean demonstration.

Just enough resemblance to keep the report moving.

McDonnell blames distortion.

Impressions from the target, the present environment, and other moments supposedly blend together.

The observer cannot tell what belongs where.

And the failed result becomes the design for Recommendation H.

Would you authorize it?

One observer in the present.

One in the immediate past.

One in the immediate future.

If all three describe the same thing, McDonnell hopes agreement will isolate the target from the distortion.

Authorize the test?

Or stop the program here?

Leave one word: AUTHORIZE or STOP.

McDonnell did not stop.

Recommendation J says participants should be intellectually prepared for possible encounters with intelligent, non-corporeal energy forms beyond ordinary space-time.

Recommendation K proposes that groups in Focus 12 build holographic patterns around sensitive areas to repel possible unwanted out-of-body presences.

The report is no longer merely asking whether headphones change attention.

It is outlining a defensive doctrine for a space whose existence has not been established.

Decades later, the modest part of the chain remains testable.

Studies of binaural beats have examined attention, anxiety, pain, and brain activity.

Results vary by method and outcome.

Some reviews find modest psychological effects.

Evidence that the brain reliably locks to the intended frequency is mixed.

Even if the sound changes a mental state, the central evidentiary gap remains.

Feeling different is not evidence of reading hidden digits.

The report provides no controlled dataset that crosses that gap.

So what survives?

The document is authentic.

Its author, date, recipient, and recommendations are visible on the page.

Travel into the Past is there.

The incomplete ten-digit result is there.

The nonphysical intelligences are there.

What is not there is proof that Gateway opened a path outside space or time.

The viral claim says the CIA proved it worked.

The real document tells a more unsettling story.

In 1983, an Army officer borrowed an audio system, a dead inventor's physical model, and a collection of anomalous experiences.

Then he built a test plan for observers in three different times.

Was that bureaucratic overreach?

Cold War desperation?

Or an unanswered anomaly wrapped in the wrong theory?

The report cannot settle that question.

But the next experiment removes the visions, the entities, and the military language.

It replaces them with zeros and ones.

Fourteen million trials.

And one unnamed person responsible for roughly half the effect.
