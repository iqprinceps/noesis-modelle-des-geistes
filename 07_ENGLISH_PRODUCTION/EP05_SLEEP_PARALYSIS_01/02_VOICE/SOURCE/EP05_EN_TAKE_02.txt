Can an experience reach us before the story that is supposed to explain it?

Years later, Hufford worked with local accounts and archives in Newfoundland.

On Fogo Island, he encountered an expression that sounded almost casual: to have the Old Hag.

A person wakes in a familiar room.

They cannot move.

Something approaches.

Breathing becomes difficult.

A weight settles onto the chest.

And through it all comes the absolute conviction that this is happening while they are awake.

For Hufford, the resemblance was uncomfortably precise.

He later collected accounts from other people who, he reported, had not known the tradition beforehand.

In 1982, that work became The Terror That Comes in the Night.

Perhaps culture gives the experience a face.

But what if the experience sometimes comes first?

Every night during REM sleep, the brain restrains much of the body's voluntary movement.

It is a protective silence built into dreaming: the dream can run while the body remains still.

Usually, awareness and movement return together.

In sleep paralysis, the handover slips out of sequence.
