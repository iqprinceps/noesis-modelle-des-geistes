Before David Hufford became a folklorist, he woke to the sound of his bedroom door opening.

Footsteps crossed the room.

His body would not move.

The mattress sank.

Weight pressed into his chest.

Years later, strangers in Newfoundland described the same sequence and gave the visitor a name Hufford said he had never heard: the Old Hag.

Researchers can make the paralysis more likely and see its traces on a monitor.

But why does a body caught between dreaming and waking so often feel as though someone else has entered the room?

Hufford later described more than fear.

He felt intention: the certainty that whatever was above him was hostile.

Then the pressure vanished.

Movement returned.

The room was ordinary again.

But the experience was too structured to feel like a random fragment of a dream: the opening door, the approaching steps, the body that would not answer, the weight on his chest.

At the time, he had no name for it.

He did not know that this sequence was already living inside another community's stories.

And that turns one frightening night into a much stranger question.
