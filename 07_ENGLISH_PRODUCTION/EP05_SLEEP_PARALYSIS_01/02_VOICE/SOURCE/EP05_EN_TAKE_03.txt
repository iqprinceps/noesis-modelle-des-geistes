Your mind reaches the room before movement reaches your muscles.

You know where you are.

You can see the door.

You can hear the room around you.

The command to move is real, but the arm does not respond.

Breathing continues, yet the chest can feel unfamiliar or restricted, especially when fear turns every sensation into urgent information.

This explains the paralysis.

It does not yet explain the footsteps.

When psychologist J. Allan Cheyne and his colleagues studied these experiences, the reports did not scatter randomly.

They repeatedly formed three broad families.

First comes the Intruder: a sound, a shadow, footsteps, the sense that someone is present.

Then the Incubus: pressure, choking, weight, something holding the body down.

And in the third family, the body itself loses its usual boundaries.

People feel themselves floating, falling, turning, or looking back at the place where their body should be.

A visitor.

An attack.

An escape from the body.

Why does one unstable border between sleep and waking keep opening into these particular rooms?
