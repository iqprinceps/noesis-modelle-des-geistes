Before David Hufford became a folklorist, he woke to the sound of his bedroom door opening.

Footsteps crossed the room.

His body would not move.

The mattress sank.

Weight pressed into his chest.

Years later, strangers in Newfoundland described the same sequence and gave the visitor a name Hufford said he had never heard: the Old Hag.

Researchers can make the paralysis more likely and see its traces on a monitor.

But why does a body caught between dreaming and waking so often feel as though someone else has entered the room?

Hufford later described more than fear.

He felt intention: the certainty that whatever was above him was hostile.

Then the pressure vanished.

Movement returned.

The room was ordinary again.

But the experience was too structured to feel like a random fragment of a dream: the opening door, the approaching steps, the body that would not answer, the weight on his chest.

At the time, he had no name for it.

He did not know that this sequence was already living inside another community's stories.

And that turns one frightening night into a much stranger question.

Can an experience reach us before the story that is supposed to explain it?

Years later, Hufford worked with local accounts and archives in Newfoundland.

On Fogo Island, he encountered an expression that sounded almost casual: to have the Old Hag.

A person wakes in a familiar room.

They cannot move.

Something approaches.

Breathing becomes difficult.

A weight settles onto the chest.

And through it all comes the absolute conviction that this is happening while they are awake.

For Hufford, the resemblance was uncomfortably precise.

He later collected accounts from other people who, he reported, had not known the tradition beforehand.

In 1982, that work became The Terror That Comes in the Night.

Perhaps culture gives the experience a face.

But what if the experience sometimes comes first?

Every night during REM sleep, the brain restrains much of the body's voluntary movement.

It is a protective silence built into dreaming: the dream can run while the body remains still.

Usually, awareness and movement return together.

In sleep paralysis, the handover slips out of sequence.

Your mind reaches the room before movement reaches your muscles.

You know where you are.

You can see the door.

You can hear the room around you.

The command to move is real, but the arm does not respond.

Breathing continues, yet the chest can feel unfamiliar or restricted, especially when fear turns every sensation into urgent information.

This explains the paralysis.

It does not yet explain the footsteps.

When psychologist J. Allan Cheyne and his colleagues studied these experiences, the reports did not scatter randomly.

They repeatedly formed three broad families.

First comes the Intruder: a sound, a shadow, footsteps, the sense that someone is present.

Then the Incubus: pressure, choking, weight, something holding the body down.

And in the third family, the body itself loses its usual boundaries.

People feel themselves floating, falling, turning, or looking back at the place where their body should be.

A visitor.

An attack.

An escape from the body.

Why does one unstable border between sleep and waking keep opening into these particular rooms?

In 1992, Japanese sleep researchers worked with sixteen healthy participants.

Their question was restrained but important: could sleep paralysis become more likely if sleep and REM were deliberately disrupted?

Participants were allowed to sleep, awakened at selected times, kept awake for an hour, and then sent back to bed while researchers monitored brain activity and muscle tone.

It did not happen on command.

It did not happen to everyone.

But under those interrupted-sleep conditions, six episodes of isolated sleep paralysis occurred.

The people knew they were in a laboratory.

They were conscious enough to recognize their surroundings, yet their bodies remained immobilized.

Most of those episodes also included disturbing visual or auditory experiences.

Their reports and polysomnograms captured the contradiction: waking awareness while muscular atonia persisted.

The doorway into the state was measurable.

What stepped through it felt deeply personal.

Because for some people, the presence arrives before the image.

That may be the strangest part of all.

One plausible explanation begins with alarm.

You are conscious enough to know that something is wrong.

You cannot move.

Your breathing feels unfamiliar.

Yet there is no visible cause.

The mind is left with danger, but no source for it.

In that condition, a perceptual system may begin searching for an agent.

A small sound gains the rhythm of a footstep.

A coat and a doorway align into a shoulder.

Pressure gains direction, then weight, then intention.

The brain does not merely record a room.

It continuously predicts what the room contains.

Under fear and incomplete information, those predictions can become intensely specific.

This offers a route by which ambiguity may acquire a body.

But it leaves a more intimate question untouched.

Why do some people feel watched before they see anything at all?

In some accounts, there is no face.

No outline.

Sometimes there is not even a sound.

There is only the certainty that another awareness is in the room.

It is waiting, observing, or moving closer.

The figure may appear later, almost as if the mind is giving shape to something it already believes is there.

Research can classify the pattern and test mechanisms behind it.

But it cannot inhabit every subjective second of the experience.

That may be why sleep paralysis never remained only a sleep disorder.

An invisible cause receives a name.

The name becomes a story.

The story changes what the next person expects to meet.

And then the encounter feeds the story again.

So return to Hufford's original problem.

His body may have been caught between REM sleep and waking.

That explains why he could not move.

The Old Hag tradition may have supplied a cultural form for similar experiences in Newfoundland.

But Hufford's question survives because he said he encountered the pattern before he encountered its name.

What if folklore does not merely invent beings?

What if, at least sometimes, it preserves the recurring shape of an experience: an experience produced where body, perception, and expectation meet?

The physiology opens the door.

Culture gives the darkness a face.

And once that face exists, it may enter the next person's night.

In 1692, a private night entered the public record.

A witness in Salem later described a woman entering a locked room, pressing onto his chest, and leaving him unable to speak or move.

Her name was Bridget Bishop.

The experience became part of the case against her.

In June, she was tried, convicted, and hanged for witchcraft.
