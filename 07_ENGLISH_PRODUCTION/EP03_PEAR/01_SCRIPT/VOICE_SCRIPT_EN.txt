For twenty-eight years, a Princeton laboratory searched for evidence that the mind could influence a machine.

It recorded roughly fourteen million trials.

Then one detail changed the meaning of the entire database.

About half of the laboratory's total excess was attributed to one person.

The person's identity was never disclosed.

The files call them Operator 10.

Who were they?

Why did so much of the result lean on them?

And what happens to a mystery when the strongest evidence belongs to someone nobody outside the room can identify?

To understand why that mattered, we have to see how small the claimed effect was.

Robert Jahn is sitting in the basement of Princeton's engineering school.

In front of him is a gray box with a red display.

He is trying to shift its output with his mind.

The machine moves by approximately one bit in ten thousand.

Too small to witness in a single run.

Large enough to consume nearly three decades.

Jahn was not an outsider to engineering.

He built propulsion systems for NASA.

He wrote a standard textbook in his field.

And as dean of Princeton's School of Engineering, he controlled appointments, funding, and reputation.

So why attach that reputation to an effect smaller than a rounding error?

The first answer involves another engineer.

James S. McDonnell founded McDonnell Douglas.

His company built the F-15, the F/A-18, and the Mercury spacecraft.

McDonnell feared that a pilot's mind might somehow interfere with the electronics of a military aircraft.

Jahn later described the idea as subjective radiation from the pilot.

That fear helped finance a laboratory at Princeton.

It was not hidden in the CIA or the Pentagon.

It operated inside the engineering school.

Princeton allowed it under two conditions.

No university money.

No graduate students.

The laboratory was called Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research.

PEAR.

Day-to-day leadership went to Brenda Dunne, a psychologist with a master's degree and no faculty appointment.

She stayed until the final day.

Years later, she summarized the project's isolation with a question.

How do you get peer review when you do not have peers?

Then they built machines.

At the center of the program was an electronic random-event generator.

A noise diode produces physical noise.

The signal is amplified and compared with a fixed threshold.

Above becomes one.

Below becomes zero.

One trial contains two hundred electronic coin tosses.

It lasts two tenths of a second.

One thousand trials form a series.

Before the series begins, the participant chooses an intention.

More ones.

Fewer ones.

Or no directional intention at all.

That last condition is the control.

If the machine drifts when nobody is trying to move it, the experiment loses its foundation.

PEAR did not prescribe a single mental technique.

Watch the display.

Look away.

Listen to music.

Concentrate.

Relax.

Some participants said effort helped.

Others said effort destroyed the effect.

The laboratory recorded the strategies without identifying a mechanism.

Then it made the invisible physical.

Nine thousand polystyrene balls fell through three hundred and thirty nylon pegs into nineteen bins.

The apparatus stood roughly three meters high.

Each run lasted about twelve minutes.

Without influence, the balls should settle into a bell curve.

The participant's task was simple to describe and absurd to perform.

Move the mountain left or right using attention alone.

The machines were dramatic.

The average claimed result was not.

Imagine ten thousand fair coin tosses.

Five thousand heads are expected.

PEAR's effect is five thousand and one.

Only when millions of trials are stacked together does a line begin to separate from zero.

And that creates a paradox.

More data should make a real signal clearer.

But when the effect is this small, every decision can also become part of the signal.

When does a run stop?

Which trials count?

Were the rules fixed before the data were seen?

Who performed the analysis?

At one bit in ten thousand, method can imitate discovery.

Then Operator 10 enters the line.

Twelve years.

Sixty-two series.

More than one hundred and twenty thousand trials in each direction.

This single operator produced about fifteen percent of the PEAR database.

An analysis published in 1994 attributed roughly half of the laboratory's total excess to that person.

Remove Operator 10 and the low-intention result falls to chance.

The high-intention result shrinks to the edge of weak statistical significance.

Robert Jahn never revealed the identity.

New Scientist described Operator 10 only as someone believed to be a member of the PEAR staff.

That leaves at least three interpretations.

Operator 10 was unusually effective.

Operator 10 interacted with the method in some unrecognized way.

Or a flexible analysis allowed one extreme contributor to carry the story.

The data alone cannot tell us which.

But PEAR then agreed to a test that could.

In the late 1990s, the Princeton team collaborated with groups in Freiburg and Giessen, Germany.

The number of trials, conditions, equipment, software, and analysis were specified in advance.

Three laboratories.

One prediction.

The machines ran.

The earlier effect did not return.

The deviations pointed in the predicted direction, but they were far too small.

Jahn and Dunne later wrote that the results missed the earlier effect by an order of magnitude.

Measured against PEAR's own findings, the prediction was rejected.

Those words came from the researchers who had produced the original claim.

That should have ended the story.

It did not.

When would you have closed the laboratory?

When Operator 10 took over the result?

When the three-laboratory replication failed?

Or not yet?

Leave one answer: OPERATOR 10, REPLICATION, or NOT YET.

PEAR kept working because another pattern changed the question.

In off-time experiments, participants directed intention toward data generated when they were not present.

Seventy-three hours before a run.

Or three hundred and thirty-six hours after it.

Then there was the baseline bind.

Control runs, in which nobody was supposed to want an outcome, contained fewer extreme deviations than ordinary statistics predicted.

The controls looked too orderly.

Critics saw a warning.

Selection or analysis might be shaping the result.

Jahn and Dunne considered a different possibility.

Perhaps a person influences the machine even without conscious intention.

Perhaps the mind is never fully neutral.

At that point, PEAR stops being a story about telekinesis.

It becomes a model of mind.

Is intention ever absent?

Does something act before we know what we want?

And if it does, can it reach backward toward data already produced?

The wider literature has not resolved those questions in PEAR's favor.

A major meta-analysis found an extremely small overall effect, then detected a pattern consistent with publication bias.

Larger studies produced smaller effects.

For most researchers, the failed three-laboratory replication outweighs an anomaly assembled from millions of flexible trials.

Still, PEAR left an unusual record.

The team published positive results.

It participated in a stricter test.

And when the test failed, it published that failure too.

In 2007, after twenty-eight years, the laboratory closed itself.

Jahn said the team had accomplished what it wanted and saw no reason to produce more of the same data.

Of Princeton's roughly seven hundred professors, none joined the project.

One outside physicist called it an embarrassment to science.

A Princeton professor said he did not believe Jahn's results, but defended his right to investigate them.

That is the difference between an institution endorsing an idea and allowing someone to pursue it with outside money.

So what survives?

The machines existed.

The data were recorded and published.

One undisclosed operator carried an extraordinary share of the anomaly.

A stricter replication failed.

For most of science, that closes the claim.

But it does not completely close the human question.

Why did an engineer who built rocket systems spend twenty-eight years pursuing one bit in ten thousand?

Was he following a signal?

A flaw?

Or the possibility that intention begins before consciousness claims it?

PEAR closed the room.

Then its random-number experiment spread across the planet as the Global Consciousness Project.

But our next file removes the machine entirely.

In Zurich, Carl Jung confronted images that seemed to possess intentions of their own.

And one of them was a black serpent he would encounter again, nineteen years later, inside somebody else's map.
