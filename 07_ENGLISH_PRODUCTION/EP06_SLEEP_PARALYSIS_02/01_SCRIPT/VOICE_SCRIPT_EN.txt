In 1692, Richard Coman told a Salem court about a night no one else could see.

He said the house was locked. A light was burning. His wife lay beside him.

Then Bridget Bishop entered the room.

Coman said she lay across his chest and pressed him down so completely that he could neither speak nor move—not even enough to wake his wife.

Years after the night itself, he put that memory under oath.

Eight days after his testimony, Bridget Bishop was hanged.

Coman's statement did not create the case against her. It entered a much larger prosecution already crowded with accusations. But it shows something extraordinary: a private experience had crossed into a courtroom, attached itself to a living woman, and become part of public reality.

The record cannot tell us what state produced Coman's night. It tells us what Salem did with it.

So how does an experience no one else can witness become evidence—and can the explanation a culture gives that experience change the next night?

Look closely at Coman's sequence.

He is awake in a familiar room. The door is locked. A figure appears. His voice fails. His body will not answer. Pressure settles across his chest.

Today, the pattern is recognizable. But Salem had no language of REM sleep or muscle atonia. It had another language ready: witchcraft.

And Salem did not invent the pattern.

In 1781, Henry Fuseli painted The Nightmare.

A woman lies thrown back across a bed. A squat figure sits on her chest. From behind a curtain, a horse stares into the room with white, impossible eyes.

The painting became famous not because it explained the night, but because it gave the night a body.

Across Europe, the nightmare was once more than a bad dream. The old word carried weight: the mare, or mara, a visitor that rides or presses the sleeper.

Christian Europe imagined the incubus. In Japan, people spoke of kanashibari—being bound as if by metal. In Newfoundland, the visitor became the Old Hag. Other traditions gave pressure, paralysis and nocturnal presence their own agents and names.

These figures do not form one clean family tree. No single demon can be followed from one language to the next.

What repeats is more basic.

The room is known. The body is trapped. Something approaches. Pressure gains intention.

Perhaps the story begins because the body produces an experience that demands a cause.

But once a culture has supplied that cause, the next person does not enter the night empty-handed.

In early modern Europe, a nocturnal attack belonged to a world of sin, spirits, curses and divine protection. If the cause was an acting being, the response had to be an action: prayer, a cross, iron, water, a household object placed near the bed.

From the outside, these acts can look irrational. From inside that world, they are practical. If the ritual brings relief, the explanation feels confirmed.

That is how the bedroom opens outward.

An episode can support a family story. A religious order. An accusation. In Salem, it could enter the machinery of a court.

The simplest modern answer would be that people see the beings their culture taught them to see.

Then David Hufford creates a problem for that answer.

Hufford studied the Old Hag tradition in Newfoundland. He also knew the experience personally. What mattered most to his work, however, were people who described the familiar pattern before they knew the local story that named it.

Paralysis. Pressure. Presence first. The Old Hag later.

Culture did not have to invent the entire event. A recurring human state could come first, and communities could build different explanations around it.

That helps explain why the same bodily structure can appear in traditions with no simple line of descent.

But it opens a harder question.

If culture does not have to create the first episode, what can it do to the second?

Imagine two people waking into the same paralysis.

One knows the state will pass.

The other has heard since childhood that this is the moment a real being attacks—and that people can die in its grasp.

The body may begin in the same place. Does the night end in the same place?

A research team led by Baland Jalal and Devon Hinton compared reports from Egypt and Denmark. Sleep paralysis appeared in both settings, but its meaning did not arrive in the same form.

In the Egyptian sample, supernatural explanations—especially jinn—were far more common. In the Danish sample, physiological explanations were more common.

These were specific samples, not portraits of two entire countries. Yet the difference inside the data was striking.

Egyptian participants in the studies reported more lifetime episodes, longer immobility and greater fear of dying during an attack. Within the Egyptian group, believing that a supernatural force caused the paralysis was associated with greater fear and longer immobility.

Association is not a one-way spell. The studies do not show that belief simply manufactures paralysis.

They show something more intimate: interpretation and bodily distress can become entangled.

Fear can fracture sleep. Fractured sleep can make another episode more likely. Another episode can feel like confirmation. The expectation waiting for the next night grows heavier.

The story has entered the loop.

And that may be the most unsettling possibility in this entire history.

A being does not have to stand physically in the room for belief in it to produce physical consequences.

So return to Richard Coman.

His body supplied wakefulness, immobility, pressure and presence. Salem supplied a name, a cause and a court willing to hear it. The experience moved outward into testimony. The testimony returned to the community as truth.

What comes first?

For one person, perhaps the experience.

For a culture, experience and story may already be braided together. Every new night meets images that are waiting. Every new account changes what the next person expects.

That is why the question is not simply whether the visitor was real.

The deeper question is what kind of reality a visitor can acquire—inside a body, inside a memory, and finally inside a room full of other people.

Which carries more power into the next night: the EXPERIENCE, or the STORY?
