In 1692, Richard Coman told a Salem court about a night no one else could see.

The room was locked. A light was burning. His wife lay beside him.

Then, he said, Bridget Bishop entered.

She lay across his chest and pressed him down. He could not speak. He could not move—not even enough to wake his wife.

Eight days after Coman gave that testimony, Bishop was hanged.

His statement did not create the case against her. It joined a much larger prosecution. We cannot diagnose his night across three centuries. The record reveals what Salem did with it: a private experience entered a courtroom and attached itself to a living woman.

How does a private experience become public truth? And can the story around it change the next night?

Coman's pattern is stark: a familiar room, waking awareness, a body that will not answer, pressure on the chest, and a presence that seems to have arrived.

Salem had no language of REM sleep or muscle atonia. It had another language ready: witchcraft.

But Salem did not invent the pattern.

In 1781, Henry Fuseli painted The Nightmare.

A woman lies thrown back across a bed. A squat figure sits on her chest. Behind the curtain, a horse stares into the room with impossible white eyes.

The painting became iconic because it gave the night a body.

Across Europe, the old mare—or mara—rode and pressed the sleeper. Christian tradition imagined the incubus. In Japan, kanashibari meant being bound as if by metal. In Newfoundland, the visitor became the Old Hag. Elsewhere, pressure and presence were understood through jinn.

These are parallels, not a demon family tree. What repeats is simpler: the room is known, the body is trapped, something approaches, and pressure gains intention.

Perhaps the body produces an experience that demands a cause. But once a culture supplies that cause, the next sleeper does not enter the dark empty-handed.

That is where folklorist David Hufford reversed the usual question.

In Newfoundland, he studied people who described the pattern before they knew the Old Hag story that named it.

Paralysis. Pressure. Presence first. The name later.

The experience could come before the story.

Culture did not need to invent the event. It could give a recurring human state a face, a motive, and a memory. Then that interpretation could wait beside the next bed.

Imagine two people waking into the same paralysis. One knows the state will pass. The other expects a being that attacks in the night.

The body may begin in the same place. Does the night end there?

Baland Jalal and Devon Hinton compared specific samples in Egypt and Denmark. These were not portraits of two countries. They were groups whose reports revealed a striking association.

Supernatural explanations, especially jinn, were more common in the Egyptian samples; physiological explanations were more common in the Danish sample. Egyptian participants also reported greater fear, longer immobility, and more lifetime episodes. Within the Egyptian group, supernatural attribution was associated with greater fear and longer paralysis.

Science makes the mystery more precise: meaning and bodily distress can become entangled.

Fear can fracture sleep. Fractured sleep can make another episode more likely. The episode can confirm the expectation. The next night arrives already charged.

Fear. Sleep. Episode. Expectation.

The story has entered the loop.

Return to Richard Coman.

His testimony contains wakefulness, immobility, pressure, and presence. Salem supplied a name, a cause, and a court willing to hear it. The experience moved outward into testimony; the testimony returned to the community as reality.

So what comes first?

For one person, perhaps the EXPERIENCE.

For a culture, EXPERIENCE and STORY may already be braided together. Every night meets images that are waiting. Every account changes what the next person expects.

Which carries more power into the next night: EXPERIENCE, or STORY?
