# Card and Visual Language Lock

This document is an adaptive quality guide for the four English editions. The
episode-specific viewer review may override any numeric preference. The new edits
are evidence-driven mystery films, not translated explainer videos.

## The governing idea

The viewer should feel that a sealed file is opening in front of them. A visual
earns its place when it does at least one useful job:

1. prove that a person, object, place, or document existed;
2. reveal one new contradiction;
3. make an invisible procedure understandable;
4. create a question a later beat can answer;
5. embody a specific mystical or emotional state that the evidence alone cannot show.

Atmosphere alone is not coverage. Information alone is not drama. The series
therefore uses a dedicated abstract layer to let the viewer feel the possibility
before returning to the evidence.

## Card rules

- Prefer one main claim, number, or question per card.
- Headline target: up to eight words; exceed only when clarity genuinely improves.
- Supporting-line target: up to sixteen words; use what the viewer needs.
- Source line: one unobtrusive line; never a paragraph.
- No four-quadrant dashboards, statistic grids, status reports, or recap decks.
- A card changes or leaves when its thought has landed; 4.5 seconds is only a
  mobile-retention warning value.
- Avoid card clusters; there is no automatic per-minute quota.
- Prefer progressive states for multi-part comparisons when they improve comprehension.
- Cards use large type that remains readable at 246 px viewport width.
- A document crop is not restyled as a fake English document. Preserve the
  original and add a clearly separate, literal translation.
- Avoid generic `SUBSCRIBE`, `YES / NO`, and evidence-score cards unless an
  episode-specific viewer test shows that one genuinely strengthens the story.

## Useful card types — not a closed list

1. **Evidence reveal** — original document crop, date, or identity.
2. **Single-number reveal** — one number whose meaning is spoken, not printed.
3. **Contradiction** — two states that replace one another on screen.
4. **Theory fork** — two or three explicitly unconfirmed interpretations.
5. **Viewer decision** — one consequential question tied to the story.

## Visual hierarchy

The preferred order is:

1. original document or authentic archive;
2. physical detail or human action;
3. visually distinct reconstruction;
4. restrained explanatory motion;
5. full-screen typography only when the words are the event.

An additional `INNER / HYPOTHESIS` layer may interrupt this hierarchy whenever
the narration enters a reported experience, unconfirmed mechanism, or competing
theory. It should be cinematic, surreal, and episode-specific.

Production metadata classifies reconstructions and speculative models. Those
classifications are not permanently displayed. A brief first-entry disclosure is
optional; visual language and source context do the continuing work.

## Mystery without misinformation

- State verified facts plainly and early.
- Attribute extraordinary claims to the person or document making them.
- After the facts, open two or three plausible interpretations.
- Show the supernatural claim vividly through symbolic or subjective imagery,
  then return to the witness, source, or proposed mechanism. The image may make
  the possibility tangible, but must not masquerade as documentary footage.
- Resolve the factual question. Leave the interpretive question alive.

## Rhythm

- Establish the episode's contradiction early enough to protect its specific hook.
- First verifiable reality anchor normally by 15 seconds; obey the episode hook map.
- Reopen attention with a question, reversal, consequence, or visual change
  before the section feels complete; 20–30 seconds is a diagnostic reference.
- First 60 seconds: usually 16–22 meaningful visual changes and no empty quota cuts.
- Shot duration follows viewer attention; 3.5–4.5 seconds is a comparison value.
- A near-silent hold is optional when it increases anticipation.
- Avoid document/card clusters when they feel repetitive; allow them when a
  progressive evidence reveal is the drama.

## Asset acceptance test

An asset is rejected if it is generic, merely decorative, visually repeats an
existing idea, contains illegible generated text, overstates a claim, or needs
the narration to explain what the viewer is looking at.

An accepted asset receives one continuous place in the episode. After the edit
leaves it, the same asset or a cosmetically altered duplicate cannot return.

The English edit may reuse language-neutral German assets only after a deliberate
`KEEP` decision in the episode lock. Everything marked `REWORK` supplies reference
or composition only. Everything marked `RETIRE` must not enter the new timeline.
