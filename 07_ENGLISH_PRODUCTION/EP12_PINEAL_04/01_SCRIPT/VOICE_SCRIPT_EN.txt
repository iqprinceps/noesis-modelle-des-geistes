There is a story about the final moment of life that fits the pineal gland almost perfectly.

When the heart stops, this small structure deep inside the brain is said to release DMT.

A molecule capable of transforming perception, space, time, and the sense of self within minutes.

Suddenly, every chapter seems to converge.

The gland Descartes placed near the soul.

The organ later transformed into the third eye.

And now an endogenous psychedelic at the boundary of death.

Then, in 2019, researchers performed an experiment in rats that made the story both more tangible and far stranger.

They measured DMT in the living brain.

They induced cardiac arrest.

And the measured signal changed.

But in some of those animals, the pineal gland had already been removed.

If this one gland were the essential source, something should disappear.

It did not.

The gland was gone.

DMT was still there.

So what is actually happening at this threshold?

DMT is not a molecule that exists only when it enters the body from outside.

Its full name is N,N-dimethyltryptamine.

Mammalian biology contains pathways through which DMT can be formed. One important enzyme in that pathway is called INMT: indolethylamine N-methyltransferase.

That immediately changes the question.

Where is DMT produced?

When does its concentration change?

What does it do at the levels found inside a living organism?

Its effects depend on location.

Concentration.

Timing.

And the measurement that catches all three.

Why, then, did the story settle so powerfully around the pineal gland?

Descartes had placed it near the soul. Theosophical writers connected it to lost inner vision. Biology revealed a gland working in darkness.

What if that same hidden organ also produced a molecule capable of opening visionary worlds?

Around the turn of the millennium, psychiatrist Rick Strassman brought DMT to a much larger audience and speculated about a possible role for the human pineal gland under extraordinary conditions, including birth and death.

The image is almost too powerful to resist.

Darkness.

Vision.

Death.

A secret gland releasing a final molecule.

But the most interesting hypotheses are the ones that can be placed under pressure.

That is what made the 2019 rat study so important.

The researchers compared animals with intact pineal glands to animals whose pineal glands had been surgically removed.

They measured extracellular DMT in the visual cortex.

And it was detectable in both groups.

With the pineal gland.

And without it.

Under those experimental conditions, the measured cortical DMT did not depend on the presence of this single organ.

The researchers also examined part of the molecular machinery involved in synthesis. The study reported INMT transcript in brain tissues outside the pineal gland, in samples examined from both rats and humans.

The center of the mystery begins to move.

Instead of asking what one secret gland contains, we can ask how widely the capacity for DMT biosynthesis may be distributed through the mammalian brain.

But how do you measure a molecule that cannot be seen moving through living tissue?

In the visual cortex of each rat, the researchers placed a fine microdialysis probe.

Think of it as a tiny chemical sampling window.

While the animal is alive, the probe collects molecules from the fluid surrounding the cells.

Those samples then enter an analytical system that separates compounds and compares the DMT signal with a reference standard.

The pinealectomized animals were allowed time to recover before baseline measurement. The removal itself was checked biochemically: the melatonin signal was absent from the dialysate.

The experiment follows a chemical trace through time.

Before cardiac arrest.

After cardiac arrest.

With the gland.

Without it.

Those four states turn a captivating idea into a question that can answer back.

With the pineal gland no longer required as the source, the molecule itself becomes more mysterious.

Which cells contribute to its formation?

What function might it have at ordinary concentrations?

Could that function change during extreme physiological stress?

To approach the final question, the researchers performed a drastic test.

They induced cardiac arrest.

Within seconds, the conditions inside the brain changed. Blood flow and oxygen delivery collapsed. Neurochemical systems entered an extraordinary state.

In this experiment, the measured DMT signal in the rat cortex rose after cardiac arrest.

Hold that finding for a moment.

In rats, cortical DMT increased after experimentally induced cardiac arrest.

Measured.

This is the observation that comes closest to the familiar story of DMT at death.

And now the control becomes decisive.

The reported increase appeared whether the pineal gland was present or absent.

A real neurochemical event remains, while the search for its source opens beyond a single gland.

The signal becomes wider.

The meaning becomes less confined.

Now imagine an instrument capable of following this transition inside a human brain.

Before an extreme state.

During it.

After it.

It would have to catch the right site, concentration, and second. And if the pineal gland played a special role, its signature would have to emerge from that sequence.

That separates four mysteries that often collapse into one sentence.

DMT exists in the body.

Where is it made?

When does it change?

And can that change shape an experience?

The rat experiment opens one route through those questions. DMT remains measurable without the pineal gland, and cardiac arrest alters the signal.

The next trail leads into the distributed chemistry of a brain under extreme conditions.

In humans, a DMT trajectory at the moment of death has not yet been measured directly.

What we have are animal observations, findings about tissue and enzymes, and human reports from the edge of consciousness.

People describing near-death experiences often speak of light, altered time, the dissolution of bodily boundaries, or spaces that feel more real than ordinary perception.

DMT can also produce radical changes in world and self. Human studies have compared the phenomenology of DMT experiences with reports of near-death states and found meaningful overlaps.

That comparison opens a genuine research direction.

The causal bridge between molecule and experience is still waiting to be mapped.

And that may be the most difficult question of all.

How do we connect a subjective event at the edge of life to a chemical signal without losing either side of the experience?

Across four episodes, this small structure has carried light, biological time, the soul, inner sight, and finally DMT.

Again and again, we search for a location.

But the biology of this series keeps moving in the other direction.

From organ to network.

Perhaps the mystery was never confined to a single gate.

Perhaps the pineal gland became the screen onto which different eras projected their greatest unanswered question.

And beneath those projections, a measurable signal remains.

Endogenous DMT has been measured. In the rat brain studied here, it remained detectable without the pineal gland. After experimental cardiac arrest, its measured cortical concentration could rise.

What that biology does, and how far it can illuminate human experiences at the boundary of life, remains open.
